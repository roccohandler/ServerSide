# The offer rebuild

> **Superseded, 2026-08-13.** This document records the three-tier / two-plan iteration
> of the offer, which the offer _simplification_ replaced with one flagship build
> ($4,900 founding / $7,500 standard) plus one optional Growth Partner plan ($299/mo),
> a Stripe payment flow and a post-deposit onboarding page. `docs/business-offer.md` is
> the authoritative record of the current model; this file is kept as the history of how
> the previous one was arrived at.

Positioning, pricing architecture, discount presentation and risk reversal — implemented,
then audited against the brief's own eighteen questions.

The short version: the site now sells **The Customer Conversion System** at three prices
instead of a website at one, the discount is real and its condition is printed beside every
figure that depends on it, and money lives in one config file that the copy interpolates
rather than restates.

Three constraints shaped every decision below and are worth stating before the detail,
because each one cost something:

1. **No fabricated price history.** The brief asked for the offer to _appear_ as a discount
   and, two sections earlier, forbade a fake reference price. Those are compatible — but
   only in one way, and it is not the way discounts are usually drawn. See §7.
2. **No manufactured scarcity.** No timer, no deadline, no "offer ends Friday". The offer is
   limited by a number and by a qualification, and both are stated as what they are.
3. **No claim about a result.** Not in the tier names, not in the value stack, not in the
   risk reversal. This site sells work it controls and says so.

## Part one — the report

The fourteen items the brief asked for, in its order.

### 1. What changed

**Positioning.** `offerName` was "The Local Lead-Ready Website System". The word doing the
damage was _website_ — it named the artefact, and the artefact is the part the buyer cares
least about. It is **The Customer Conversion System** now. That is not a cosmetic rename:
$4,900 for a website invites comparison with a template, and the same figure for the system
that turns visitors into calls invites comparison with a month of advertising. The second
comparison is the one that makes it look cheap.

**Money became data.** `apps/client/src/config/pricing.ts` is new and is the only file that
states a figure as a number. Everything else — three pricing surfaces, the hero, the entry
paths, the commercial terms, the cancellation copy — interpolates it. Savings are derived,
never typed. Deposits are computed. The set of legal currency strings is generated by
`sanctionedFigures()`, so the guard that fails the build on an unsanctioned `$` figure now
derives its allow-list from the same source the copy does.

**Three tiers instead of one price.** Foundation / **Growth** / Dominate, ordered by price
with the recommended one in the middle, and a test that fails if that stops being true.

**A founding-client discount that is defensible.** Covered in §7 below, because it is the
part most likely to be got wrong.

**A risk reversal that can be checked.** `launchStandard` — eight pass/fail conditions a
buyer could verify on their own site the day it goes live, plus a promise about the work and
an explicit statement of what it does not promise. "Satisfaction guaranteed" transfers no
risk because neither party can tell whether it has been met.

**The assessment now leads somewhere.** `recommendedTier()` turns twenty scored answers into
one recommendation, and the top band recommends nothing at all.

**Fourteen defects found by auditing this work rather than the old work**, across three passes — an adversarial review, the brief's own eighteen questions, and a sweep for drift from the renames. Recorded in §13.

### 2. Sections kept

Everything that was already carrying an argument. `SystemSection` (the ten components),
`ManagementSection`, `LaunchSection`, `GuaranteeSection`, `CancellationSection`,
`ConversionSection`, `OpportunitySection`, `EvidenceSection`, `DemoSection`,
`TrustSection`, `ReviewOfferSection`, the examples grid and the FAQ. The offer rebuild
changed what `OfferSection` renders; it did not restructure the page around it, because the
page order had already been decided by the previous pass on evidence this one had no reason
to overturn.

### 3. Sections removed

None, in this pass. The consolidation happened in the value-per-second pass — see
`docs/VALUE-PER-SECOND.md` §5 — and removing more to make room for pricing would have been
undoing that work rather than continuing it.

One thing was deleted: the `care_plan_selected` analytics event, declared and never fired.
There is no recurring-plan call to action to fire it, deliberately — a fourth identical
button on the same screen buys nothing. A declared event nothing emits is indistinguishable
from an event nobody triggers once a provider is connected.

### 4. Sections consolidated

**The care plans became a component.** `CarePlans` renders the recurring half of the offer
and is used by both pricing surfaces. It was JSX inside `OfferSection`, and the consequence
is in §13.

**The value stack absorbed the three "ways to start" cards** — that happened in the previous
pass, and this one confirmed the decision: three entry cards directly above three pricing
tiers made the reader choose twice.

### 5. Final offer structure

Problem → outcome → mechanism → proof → value stack → **founding condition** → price →
recurring plan → terms → risk reversal → CTA.

Risk reversal sits _above_ the price on the homepage, which was the previous pass's largest
structural change and is unchanged here. Somebody who decides $4,900 is too much never
reaches material written to answer that objection if it is printed below the number.

The value stack is four groups — build the foundation, turn visitors into enquiries, keep
improving it, keep you out of the weeds — each stating its deliverables and then answering
"why should the owner of a plumbing business care". No per-component dollar values are
published, for the reason in §14.

### 6. Final pricing structure

| Tier                       | Standard | Founding | For                                              |
| -------------------------- | -------- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Foundation                 | $3,500   | $2,500   | a business with no website, or one beyond fixing |
| **Growth** _(recommended)_ | $7,500   | $4,900   | most businesses; the tier the page argues toward |
| Dominate                   | $12,000  | $8,500   | multiple service areas and a longer service list |

Half to start, half on the day it goes live. Both halves are computed from the tier price,
so a price change cannot leave a stale deposit figure behind.

After launch, sold separately and never bundled:

| Plan                | Monthly | Annual |
| ------------------- | ------- | ------ |
| **Growth Partner**  | $299    | $2,990 |
| Growth Partner Plus | $499    | —      |

A project that only makes financial sense with a subscription attached is a project priced
dishonestly, and the buyer works that out in month four. `carePricing.optOut` says on the
page that you can take the project and stop.

### 7. How the discount is represented

**This is where the brief's two requirements meet, and the resolution is not the obvious
one.**

§9 asked for the reference price with a strike-through. §8 forbade a fake reference price
and cited the FTC. Under **16 CFR 233.1** (Guides Against Deceptive Pricing) a former-price
comparison is defensible only where the former price was actually charged, publicly, on a
regular basis, for a reasonably substantial period. **This business has never charged
$3,500, $7,500 or $12,000.** They are a rate card being established now.

A strike-through is how a page says "was" without typing it. So there is none, anywhere:

- The standard price is rendered **small, above the asking price, and labelled** "Standard
  project price" — a concurrent price, which is what it is.
- The saving is `standard − founding`, computed, never typed.
- The condition is printed above the tiers: the first ten projects are priced below the
  rate card **in exchange for permission to document the work as a case study**. That is a
  real qualification a buyer either accepts or does not.
- The count is honest arithmetic — "10 of 10 still open" — and `remaining` is a number in a
  config file that has to be decremented by hand as projects are sold.

Three guards hold it:

- `content.test.ts` bans the _words_ that would make it a claim about the past — `was $`,
  `normally`, `regularly`, `usually` next to a figure.
- `HomePage.test.tsx` and `ServicesPage.test.tsx` ban the _markup_ that would make the same
  claim without any words: `<s>`, `<del>`, `<strike>`.
- A third test bans a deadline the business has not committed to.

**No timer. No countdown. No "ends Friday".** The offer is limited by a number and a
qualification, and it says so.

### 8. How pricing is centralised

`apps/client/src/config/pricing.ts` holds prices as **numbers**, formatted on the way out by one
`Intl.NumberFormat`. It exports:

- `projectTiers`, `carePlans` — the data, with `order`, `recommended`, `includes`, `terms`
- `currentPrice(tier)` / `hasFoundingDiscount(tier)` — which price applies, and whether a
  discount may be claimed at all
- `saving(tier)`, `deposit(tier)` — derived
- `foundingOffer` — one `enabled` flag that removes the discount, the condition, the count
  and every sentence that mentions any of them
- `recommendedTier(score, outOf)` — the assessment's bridge into the offer
- `sanctionedFigures()` — every legal currency string on the site, generated

Changing $4,900 to $5,900 is one number. The brief asked for exactly that, and it is now
true in a stronger sense than intended: the copy interpolates, so the sentences change too.

### 9. How the assessment connects to the offer

It used to dead-end. Somebody scored twenty checks, read five findings written in their own
customer's language, and reached the end of the best asset on the site with no link to
anything.

`AuditDiagnosis` now takes the total and the points available and calls `recommendedTier`:

- below ~65% → **Growth**, the rebuild
- 65–85% → **Foundation**, because the bones are there
- above ~85% → **nothing.** The honest answer is that a rebuild is not what is holding them
  back, and the section says so instead of recommending a purchase.

The recommendation links to `/services#offer`, and `outline.test.tsx` now proves that anchor
resolves on that page — see §13, because for a while it did not.

The fraction is computed from the points available rather than assumed to be forty, so
adding a category to the audit cannot silently re-band everybody who has already taken it.
A test pins all three branches, including the one that recommends nothing: a recommender
that always recommends buying is not a recommender.

### 10. Analytics

The seam only — `track()` is a no-op until a provider is connected, and connecting one
requires updating `content/legal.ts`, which currently states truthfully that this site does
no analytics tracking.

Added or changed by this pass:

| Event                   | Carries        | Answers                                                  |
| ----------------------- | -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| `offer_viewed`          | —              | did they reach the value stack                           |
| `pricing_viewed`        | `page`         | did they reach the price, and on which surface           |
| `pricing_tier_selected` | `tier`, `from` | **which tier people actually reach for**                 |
| `care_plans_viewed`     | `location`     | did they get past the price to what happens after launch |
| `guarantee_viewed`      | —              | is the risk reversal reached before the decision         |

`pricing_tier_selected` is the one that matters. If nobody ever clicks Foundation it is not
an entry point, it is an anchor — and that is a pricing decision, not a copy one.

`CtaLocation` was also made real. Seven values were declared and fourteen were in use, and
nothing noticed, because the general `track` signature accepted any string record. It is now
enforced by an overload, with the permissive signature excluded from `cta_clicked` — without
that exclusion the constraint compiles away to nothing, which is how it got into that state.

### 11. Tests run

`npm run verify` — Prettier, ESLint, TypeScript on both workspaces, the full Vitest suite,
both builds, the SEO page generation and the payload budget.

### 12. Test results

**462 passed, 30 files, exit 0.** 17 pages built. Eager JS 457.3 kB (138.1 kB gzipped)
against a 465 kB / 142 kB budget; CSS 88.0 kB (13.9 kB gzipped) against 95 kB / 15 kB.

No test was deleted. Nine failed after the rename and the CTA change, and all nine were
fixed by reading the label from the content layer rather than by pinning the new wording —
those tests were asserting that a specific sentence existed, when what they meant to assert
was that the button says whatever the content says.

Guards added by this pass, each negative-controlled by injecting a real violation and
confirming the failure:

- founding price never presented as a former price (words, and markup, separately)
- no manufactured deadline
- every saving derived from two real prices; no tier priced above its own standard
- tiers ordered by price with the recommended one in the middle
- `recommendedTier` scales with the number of categories, and returns null for a good site
- no duplicate class definitions at the top level of one CSS module
- **no CSS rule that a later, wider-matching media query always beats**
- no CSS module class that nothing renders
- no component publishing a founding price without the standard one beside it
- every cross-page `#fragment` link resolves on the page it points at
- the free diagnostic is never called by its old name anywhere in the content layer
- both bodies of the delivery email name the offer the same way

### 13. Remaining limitations

**Fourteen defects in my own work, found by auditing it.** Recorded here rather than quietly
fixed, because the pattern is the useful part.

Four were found by an adversarial multi-agent pass over the finished code — 41 findings
raised, 36 refuted, 5 confirmed:

- **A three-column pricing rule that never applied.** `.priceList` was set to three columns
  in a `64rem` block, and to two columns in a `48rem` block 837 lines further down. Both
  match at desktop widths; equal specificity means the later one wins. Three tiers rendered
  two-plus-an-orphan **at every desktop width**, in `Offer.module.css`.
- **A class-name collision.** CSS modules scope per _file_, not per rule. A new care-plan
  grid reused `.careList`, a name `ManagementSection` and `GrowthSection` were already
  rendering, and silently reformatted both into two columns above 48rem.
- **"A page per service area, built to rank in each one"** — a promised search result, in
  copy I wrote, on a site whose FAQ answers "do you guarantee Google rankings" with a flat
  no.
- **"Starts losing… from about month three"** — an invented decay timeline. Replaced with
  things that observably happen: services change, certificates expire, forms break without
  telling anybody.

Two more were found by running the brief's own eighteen questions against the finished site
rather than against my memory of it:

- **`/services` published all three founding prices with no condition anywhere on the page**
  — while the homepage carried the block explaining it, and the section's own comment
  claimed "the two pages cannot disagree". True of the numbers, false of the thing that
  makes the numbers truthful. It is the page somebody reaches from a search for what a
  managed website costs, so it is the one most likely to be read _instead of_ the homepage.
- **The same dead-CSS defect, in the second file.** `Services.module.css` had the identical
  64rem-before-48rem ordering. The duplicate-selector guard written for the first one could
  not see it, because the two rules are in different media blocks and one is in a comma
  group. There is now a guard for the pattern itself.

And a third pass — sweeping every surface for drift from the two renames rather than
trusting that they had propagated — found the offer being called two different things in
four places, one of them inside a single email:

- **The delivery email said "the free review" in its text body and "the free assessment" in
  its HTML body** — the same sentence, in the same message. Which one a subscriber reads
  depends on their mail client. Neither test suite could see it, because each body was only
  ever asserted against on its own.
- **The test guarding that email was passing vacuously.** It capped mentions of the paid
  service by counting the phrase "free review", so once the offer was renamed the assertion
  was satisfied by the phrase being absent entirely. A cap that counts the wrong words is
  not a cap.
- **Step one of the published launch process still read "Free website review"**, and the
  hero's own trust points promised a "free review" — while the primary button on the same
  screen offered a free _assessment_. `site.ts` warns about exactly this failure in a
  comment, which turns out not to be a mechanism. Both interpolate the name now.
- **`docs/business-offer.md` still described the offer by its old name** in §2, the
  positioning statement, in the file marked authoritative — along with a cross-reference
  pointing at the wrong section.
- **The guard written to catch all of that was one word too narrow.** It banned "free
  review" and "free website review", and four pieces of rendered copy called the product
  **"the review"** — including the contact form's email hint, which read "Where I will send
  the review" directly beneath a button offering an assessment. The phrase is banned
  outright now, which forces the other meaning of the word to be written as "the customer
  review". One rule with no exemption list beats a rule with one, and the disambiguated
  sentence is clearer anyway.

Plus one broken link and two pieces of dead code:

- **`/services#offer` scrolled nowhere.** The assessment's recommendation — the link the
  whole §9 bridge exists to produce — pointed at an anchor that was rendered on the
  homepage. The existing guard checked that every declared section id is rendered
  _somewhere_, which is not the same question.
- **An orphaned annual figure.** `/services` priced the annual prepay of a plan it never
  showed: "Or pay annually: $2,990 per year", directly beneath three project tiers, where
  the natural reading is that it is an annual alternative to the project.
- **Six dead CSS rules** left behind when the annual panel moved inside the card it prices.

**What is still weak, and not fixable in a repository:**

- **No social proof of any kind.** No testimonials, no case studies, no client count, no
  years in business. The site says so plainly, which is the best available answer and is not
  as good as having some. The founding-client offer is designed to produce the first one.
- ~~**No live demo.**~~ **Resolved after this pass** — five demonstration sites now run
  inside the application at `/demo/<trade>`. They are fictional businesses with no
  fabricated proof of any kind, which is the constraint that made them publishable at all.
  See `docs/DEMO-SITES-PLAN.md`.
- **No measurement.** Every claim in this document about what converts better is a
  hypothesis until a provider is connected.
- **Mobile is asserted structurally, not measured.** Layouts are single-column by default
  and widen at breakpoints; the heading outline, the anchors and the cascade are tested. No
  test opens a real browser at 360 px, and the two column-count defects above are exactly
  what that would have caught first.

### 14. What needs the owner's confirmation

Recorded in full in `docs/business-offer.md` §17. The three that block publication:

1. **Are the standard prices real?** $3,500 / $7,500 / $12,000 must be the prices actually
   charged to a client who does not qualify for founding pricing. If the intention is that
   nobody ever pays them, they are a fictional reference price and every one of them has to
   come off the site — the presentation is defensible only because both prices are live.
2. **Is the cap of ten real?** "The first 10 projects" must mean the offer ends at ten.
   `remaining` is decremented by hand; publishing "10 of 10 still open" while quietly
   serving the eleventh is the same defect as a countdown timer that resets.
3. **Is case-study permission genuinely required?** It is the condition that makes the lower
   price a discount rather than a discount-shaped decoration. If a founding client can
   decline and keep the price, the qualification is not one.

Also unconfirmed, and smaller: the $499 plan's scope and price split, the per-tier service
page counts (four on Foundation, eight on Growth — scope commitments), and all five names.
"The Dominate package" is a thing somebody has to say on the phone.

**No per-component dollar values are published in the value stack.** The brief asked for
them and conditioned them on being defensible; there is no defensible basis for "conversion
copywriting: $1,200" here. The mechanism is built the same way `demoUrl` is, so real figures
can be added the day they exist.

## Part two — the final audit

The brief's eighteen questions, answered against the finished site. A self-audit that
answers eighteen out of eighteen with "yes" is not an audit, so where the answer is qualified
it is written as qualified.

**1. Can a service-business owner understand what is being sold within 5 seconds?**
Yes. Eyebrow: "Managed websites for local service businesses". Heading: "Turn your website
into your best salesperson." Both are on the first screen with the price.

**2. Is the desired business outcome obvious?**
Yes — "so more of the people who find you online end up calling you". Written as calls
rather than as revenue, because calls are what a website can affect and revenue is not.

**3. Is the offer clearly different from "someone builds websites"?**
Yes, and it is the section immediately after the hero that does it: "You don't need another
website." The differentiator is ongoing ownership — build it, maintain it, improve it —
stated as two lanes side by side, where the usual way ends at "you're on your own".

**4. Is Value Per Second clearly communicated?**
**Deliberately not as a slogan.** The value-per-second brief's own warning was against
building a bloated agency website that talks endlessly about how efficient it is. It shows
up as what the site _is_ — a homepage that lost six sections and 23 of 31 FAQ answers to the
page written for depth, a payload budget that fails the build, motion that only staggers
inside an ordered list — and as an outcome the customer feels: "opens quickly on a phone in
a driveway, not just on office wi-fi." That is the right form for it. Judge this one by the
page's length, not by its copy.

**5. Does the offer feel substantially more valuable than a generic website?**
Yes. Ten components across two phases, four value-stack groups each answering "what is this
for", four things "included, not sold separately", and eight checkable launch conditions.
The weight is in what keeps happening after launch, which is the half a generic website
quote does not contain at all.

**6. Is Growth clearly the best-value option?**
Yes — badge, brand border, shadow, middle position, and a test that fails if it stops being
the middle price. The emphasis is a border rather than a scale transform so the three prices
stay comparable at a glance. It reads correctly on both pricing surfaces now; until this
audit it had no emphasis at all on `/services`.

**7. Is the current price obvious?**
Yes. Largest type on the card, brand colour, and the standard price above it is deliberately
small and muted. The number a reader leaves with is what they pay.

**8. Is the reference price understandable and truthful?**
Yes, **conditional on item 14.1.** Labelled rather than struck through, presented as a
concurrent price rather than a former one, with the qualifying condition above the tiers and
restated beside every figure published away from them. If the standard prices are not real
prices, the answer to this question becomes no and the fix is deletion, not rewording.

**9. Is the savings amount automatically calculated?**
Yes. `saving(tier) = tier.standard − tier.founding`, asserted, with a second assertion that
no tier is priced above its own standard — which would advertise a negative discount.

**10. Is the CTA low-friction?**
Yes. "Get my free website assessment", not "Buy now" and not "Book a call". The first
conversion is never "decide whether to spend $4,900". The three tier buttons all lead to the
same place, because nobody decides to spend four thousand dollars from a pricing card — what
the button buys is finding out which tier they need.

**11. Does the assessment logically lead into the offer?**
Yes, now. See §9. The honest caveat: the site has two free diagnostics with similar names —
the self-scored **Website Revenue Audit** at `/audit` and the **free website assessment** you
request on the contact page. They are genuinely different products with different friction,
and the naming does not make that obvious. Flagged as an owner call rather than renamed
unilaterally; a live product name is a positioning decision.

**12. Is there enough proof without fabricating anything?**
**This is the weakest answer on the page, and it is the honest one.** There are no clients,
so there are no testimonials, no case studies and no client logos — and none have been
invented. What exists instead: five demonstration sites, a public teardown, cited category
research with every advertising benchmark labelled as an advertising benchmark, eight
checkable launch conditions, and a free diagnosis that demonstrates the reasoning rather than
describing it. Methodology and transparency are what this site has in place of proof. They
are not the same thing, and it says so.

**13. Is risk reduced?**
Yes, and in more places than the guarantee. The Launch Standard is checkable; the guarantee
is explicitly about work rather than results; cancellation terms are published before they
are needed; the domain, hosting and content are in the client's name from the start; there
is no annual contract; prepaid months are refunded; and the recurring plan can be declined
entirely. The site publishes the way out as plainly as the way in.

**14. Is the recurring service clearly separated from the initial project?**
Yes, now. Separate block, subordinate to the project prices, "starts after launch, never
before", and an explicit opt-out. Until this audit, `/services` separated them so thoroughly
that it showed the annual price of a plan it never described.

**15. Does the mobile experience work?**
**Qualified yes.** Every grid is single-column by default and widens at breakpoints; the
price cards stretch to full width; the standard-price row wraps; the CTA is a block button.
Two real column-count defects were found in the cascade during this pass, so the honest
answer is that mobile is _structurally_ sound and _not measured_. A real browser at 360 px is
the check that has not been run.

**16. Is every section earning its place?**
Yes on the homepage, which lost six sections and 23 FAQ answers in the previous pass and
gained none in this one. `OfferSection` is the longest section on the site and it stays that
way: value stack, included, price, plan, terms. Splitting it across three sections is how a
reader loses the thread of what they are buying. The three "ways to start" cards that used to
sit directly above the three pricing tiers are the clearest cut that was made — the reader
was being asked to choose twice.

**17. Does the page feel premium rather than cheap?**
Yes, by restraint rather than by decoration: no timers, no fake urgency, no strike-throughs,
no exclamation marks, no stock photography of somebody in a hard hat pointing at a tablet.
Published terms and a stated boundary — "where my job ends" — read as expensive in a way that
adjectives do not.

**18. Does the page make $4,900 feel like a rational business investment rather than a
generic website expense?**
This is the question the whole structure exists to answer, and the argument arrives in the
right order: what one customer is worth → where the leak is → the six steps the website owns
and the three it does not → what gets built → what keeps happening → what "finished" means →
_then_ the number. By the time $4,900 appears the reader has been given a way to compare it
with something other than a template.

Two things would move it further, and neither is a copy change: **one real client who agrees
to be named**, and **one demo that can be clicked**. Both are the owner's to produce. Until
then the strongest honest answer is the arithmetic in the audit, which uses the reader's own
numbers and shows its working — including the "I don't know" branches, which produce fewer
lines rather than invented ones.

## What this pass did not do

- **No commercial decision was changed on my own initiative.** Prices, terms, the minimum
  term, the guarantee and the scope quantities came from `docs/business-offer.md` and go back
  to it. The three-tier structure and the founding-client mechanism are new and are flagged
  for confirmation rather than assumed.
- **No guard was weakened to make new copy pass.** The currency sweep was extended
  deliberately — it derives its allow-list from the pricing config now — and the payload
  budget was raised once, by 10 kB, with the measurement and the justification recorded
  inline at the point of change.
- **No statistic was added.** The evidence layer is unchanged. Every borrowed number still
  states what it does not show, and advertising benchmarks are still labelled as advertising
  benchmarks rather than as website conversion benchmarks.
